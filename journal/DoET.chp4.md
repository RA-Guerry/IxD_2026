## Constraints
Constraints, despite what they sound like, are actually tools which people can use to tell what an object is supposed to do, or how to put together parts into a greater whole. As **Page 125** details before elaborating on the types of constraints, they're "powerful clues, limiting the set of possible actions. The thoughtful use of constraints in design lets people readily determine the proper course of action, even in a novel situation." For example, physical constraints directly prevent you from taking an action. When used ideally, it should be obvious how a person can or can't physically interact with something. This is one of 4 types of constraint. The other types are cultural constraints, when we've been taught not to do something; semantic, or situationally relevant constraints; and logical constraints. Each of these constraints is a powerful tool to use or understand when making a design, and they're very useful to review and give a name to. It's also important to note when a design lacking one of these constraints and will need to be compensated for. For example, designs aimed at any audience of young kids have to acknowledge the child may not yet have much knowledge of cultural constraints like do not drink cleaning products, and physically preventing them from doing something like opening the cleaning cabinet may be necessary.

## Switch Difficulty 
On **Page 34-36**, and well into the later pages, Norman describes notable trouble with switches, especially in environments where many are necessary. He describes being unable to figure out a set of light switches, similar to these ones: 
![A row of six light switches, unlabeled](https://thumbs.dreamstime.com/b/closeup-multiple-light-switches-grouped-together-all-flipped-multiple-light-switches-grouped-together-114664571.jpg)

These indicate a lack of affordance, signifiers, or constraints. While Norman argues for the layout of the switches to indicate where they go, a solid horizontal panel is arguably the most convenient in terms of physical movement to operate every switch at a given moment's notice and how much space it occupies. The lack of indicators corresponding the switches to the lights seems the bigger problem. One problem would be a simple label to the switches, for a strong signifier. Another more system wide change, which would struggle against the Legacy problem Norman described earlier, would be to standardize light switches so there's a cultural indicator to them, such as the leftmost switch corresponding to the light closest, and so on and so forth. There's no perfect solution that preserves all the benefits of the current design while improving on its effectiveness.
