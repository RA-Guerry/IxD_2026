## Constraints
Constraints, despite what they sound like, are actually tools which people can use to tell what an object is supposed to do, or how to put together parts into a greater whole. As **Page 125** details before elaborating on the types of constraints, they're "powerful clues, limiting the set of possible actions. The thoughtful use of constraints in design lets people readily determine the proper course of action, even in a novel situation." For example, physical constraints directly prevent you from taking an action. When used ideally, it should be obvious how a person can or can't physically interact with something. This is one of 4 types of constraint. The other types are cultural constraints, when we've been taught not to do something; semantic, or situationally relevant constraints; and logical constraints. Each of these constraints is a powerful tool to use or understand when making a design, and they're very useful to review and give a name to. It's also important to note when a design my lack one of these constraints and will need to be compensated for. For example, designs aimed at any audience of young kids have to acknowledge the child may not yet have much knowledge of cultural constraints like do not drink cleaning products, and physically preventing them from doing something like opening the cleaning cabinet may be necessary.
